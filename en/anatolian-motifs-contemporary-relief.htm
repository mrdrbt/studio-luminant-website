Anatolian Motifs
in Cast Relief

The three circles of çintemani, the saz leaves of İznik, the star geometry of Seljuk stonework: carrying them into a room as depth that light reads, not as a flat pattern.

Studio Luminant · Heritage & Design · 8 min read

Anatolian ornament is back in contemporary interiors: the three circles of çintemani, the tulips and saz leaves of İznik tile, the star geometry carved into Seljuk stonework. Most routes into a room flatten it, though: a printed wallpaper, a painted surface. The pattern arrives, and what kept these motifs alive for centuries, their depth and the play of light and shadow across it, is left behind. This article looks at reading Anatolian motifs as cast relief that light reads, instead of a flat quotation.

Not Only Motif: Depth

None of these traditions was flat. Tile was read by light moving over glaze; kalem work was layered; Seljuk stonework is relief by definition. The usual mistake in a contemporary reproduction is to copy the image of the motif and drop its relief. The result has the right pattern and little of its character.

Studio Luminant reads a motif as a depth system, not a picture: which lines rise, which recede, which shadows form when light arrives across the surface at a low angle. An architectural relief panel carries the motif in its body, so it is read afresh as the angle of light changes through the day. Here heritage is not decoration; it is the texture of the room.

Çintemani: Three Circles, One Rhythm

Çintemani is an Ottoman motif of three circles, usually set in a triangle and often accompanied by a pair of wavy lines. It was used as a sign of strength and protection, on sixteenth-century kaftans and on İznik ceramics alike. Rhythmic and repeatable by construction, it suits a continuous cast surface.

As relief, the circles become raised forms that catch low light, and the same wall reads differently in the morning and the evening. This is not a printed pattern but a rhythm set into the surface: calm, repeating, in a single tone. The strength of çintemani lies in that restraint. One motif can carry a whole wall.

İznik: Turning the Brush Stroke into Shadow

The İznik repertoire (tulip, carnation, hyacinth, saz leaf, rumi and hatayi) was made with brush and glaze, in cobalt, turquoise and the well-known coral red. Carrying it into relief does not mean imitating tile; it is a reinterpretation. Instead of copying colour, take the line and curl of a saz leaf and give it physical depth, so that the leaf is read by its shadow rather than its colour.

The translation draws on stylised simplicity, not photographic realism. In a single mineral tone on a matte surface, the pattern stands up as relief by itself. İznik’s richness moves out of colour and into light.

Seljuk Geometry: Already a Relief Language

Anatolian Seljuk ornament, with its interlocking systems of stars and polygons, was worked in stone, brick and tile in centres such as Konya, Sivas and Divriği. The geometry was already three-dimensional. Casting it as relief respects the medium it came from instead of flattening it.

Its regular repeat sits naturally with panel layouts. The precision of the interlace, on the other hand, calls for high texture resolution, and that is where digital modelling and careful casting earn their place. Lumina PMAG™ is used for interior surfaces, including complex, detail-critical transitions.

Avoiding Pastiche

With historical motifs the real risk is pastiche, a slide into kitsch. What prevents it is a discipline: reinterpret the motif’s logic (its rhythm, its proportion, how light travels across it) instead of photocopying the motif itself.

In practice it comes down to three decisions. Stylisation: an abstracted line, not a literal one. Restraint: one motif, one tone, matte rather than gloss. Scale shift: enlarge and abstract the motif until it reads as an architectural surface and not as a souvenir. Handled this way, the Anatolian trace stops being a quotation and becomes the room’s own language.

Material and Light

The physical counterpart of the reading is built in material and light. Studio Luminant makes its relief panels in Lumina PMAG™ — Polymer Modified Composite Gypsum, a mineral cast material, for interiors including column cladding, counter fronts and feature surfaces. Panels are hand-cast, poured into silicone moulds and left to set. The surface comes paint-ready and is painted on site in a warm mineral palette (cream, caramel, terracotta, amber or a mineral green accent), matte rather than gloss.

What is finally delivered is light. A relief surface depends entirely on the angle at which light arrives: lit flat from the front, its depth disappears; lit at a graze, it takes on character. For that reason a lighting-angle note goes with every panel, so the shadow depth the architect expects at design stage is kept once the wall is lit on site.

A Decision Made per Project

The Anatolian trace is a design-stage decision: which motif, how far to abstract it, which format and area, which material. It follows the same Bespoke route as every other job: brief and context, motif development, material choice, master approval, format and m², surface finish, technical data sheet, installation drawing and fixing.

For heritage-based relief the master approval matters most. Whether a motif has slipped into pastiche or stands as a contemporary reading only becomes clear under real light, on the physical master.

Practical Note

On a heritage-motif surface, see the master before casting and judge it under a raking light. The depth of a relief does not read fully from a drawing or an image, and a raking light is a good way to decide whether the motif is a contemporary reading or a copy.
